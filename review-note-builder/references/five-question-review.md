# Five-question analysis note guide

Use this guide for Stage 1: build an analysis note that can become the backbone of a review PPT. Read the paper before judging. For every question, say what the paper establishes as well as what remains uncertain. Distinguish a false claim, insufficient explanation, an untested alternative explanation, and an optional extension. Do not promote a tentative PPT reaction into a confirmed flaw. Reconstruct the authors' actual claim and terminology before applying the reviewer's interpretation; record the source location and any scope or statistical qualifier that would change the judgment.

1. **Problem definition.** Explain in direct language the scenario, object, difficulty, intended improvement, and important constraints. State the problem as a declarative condition, not as a “how to” solution question. Check consistency among the problem, objective, assumptions, and method. Distinguish an unclear definition from a clearly defined narrow scope, and both from a conclusion that exceeds the evidence. A narrow, meaningful subproblem is not itself a flaw.
2. **Why it matters.** Judge whether the loss, limitation, or capability gap is consequential in the authors' intended scenario. Follow the causal explanation in the paper and examine any motivating measurements or cases. Production statistics or a separate motivation experiment are not mandatory; ask for more evidence only if relevance, magnitude, or practical value could change the review. Do not use the proposed method's improvement as a substitute for motivation.
3. **Existing work.** Identify the closest approaches, what they already solve, their strengths and conditions, and the specific remaining gap. Difference alone is not failure. Distinguish a different objective or scope from a genuine capability limitation; identify work that is complementary. Check decisive characterizations against primary papers or official sources, and explain what a fair comparison would isolate. Do not demand unrelated baselines or infer deliberate omission from citation age alone.
4. **Key idea.** Explain how the core insight addresses the surviving gap, then assess whether its assumptions and causal logic make sense. Give enough of the design path to judge the idea: inputs/state, admission or decision rule, execution, outputs, feedback, and relevant costs. A combination of known mechanisms can contribute; assess the new capability and integration rather than requiring a new algorithm. Separate what the paper demonstrates from plausible explanations and untested failure hypotheses.
5. **Experiment plan and evidence.** Ask whether the evidence supports the central claims and tested scope. Check setup, baselines, metrics, protocol, attribution of gains, and relevant costs against the paper and appendices. For each needed additional experiment, state **what is missing, what question it would test, and why the answer could change the conclusion**. Request only the smallest discriminative test; mark broader coverage or polish as optional. Do not demand more models, hardware, tasks, production statistics, or significance tests by default.

Within Question 5, include enough experimental setup to orient the audience, then map each figure/table used in the supplied PPT to its purpose, comparison, reported conclusion, and evidence limit where material. Preserve the authors' terminology and intended purpose. A schematic is not a measurement; a case study is not an aggregate result; a development set is not held-out evaluation. This map explains the evidence used in the review—it is not a substitute for the Question 5 judgment, nor a license to turn every plot into a criticism.

6. **Writing, terminology, and presentation details.** Check factual wording, terminology, equations, tables, citations, figure labels, and cross-references. Separate errors or reasoning problems that could change the claims, review assessment, or talk from minor polish. Give exact locations, the effect, and a concise correction. A mismatch in a prior-work characterization can affect novelty assessment; a typo may not. Keep major concerns visibly separate from minor fixes.

After the six review parts, provide a concise overall assessment. Tie the tentative recommendation to the actual strengths and concerns, and preserve a user- or teacher-supplied verdict as their judgment. The overall assessment is a synthesis, not a seventh review question.

Under each of Questions 1–6, include the reviewer's actual assessment, not only a neutral explanation of the paper. State clearly when an aspect is adequate; do not manufacture a criticism to fill a section. For each substantial issue use **judgment → manuscript evidence → effect → smallest necessary clarification, correction, or test**. Keep author claims, measured observations, and reviewer inferences distinct. Useful precision checks include: written program branches are not the same as open-ended adaptation to new evidence; an opportunity to catch an error does not guarantee detection; successful execution is not semantic correctness; an end-to-end comparison may conflate several mechanisms; and point estimates, development results, or schematic diagrams have distinct evidence limits. Apply only those relevant to the paper.

When a PPT is supplied as the user's final delivery, use its actual problem statement and explanation depth as the note's target, while independently checking every claim against the paper. Do not merely copy the deck or obey provisional speaker-note prompts. Supplement material review findings that the PPT missed, clearly separating them from the authors' claims and from the user's conclusions. Stage 2 formal review is a separate invocation using the user's post-presentation PPT, discussion conclusions, and revised analysis note.
