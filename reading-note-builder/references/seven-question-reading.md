# Seven-question speaking and evidence guide

Use these questions to build an explanation the user can present and defend. The first five follow problem-formulation and method-design reasoning: define and test the research problem, then derive and explain the method against the surviving challenge. Adapt the amount of space to the paper and supplied PPT; do not force equal-length sections.

1. **What is the problem?** Present the page as **background plus problem**. State the top-level condition or limitation as a declarative sentence and give enough context to make it concrete. Keep desired improvement, cause, and proposed solution separate. Do not add a generic standalone “problem boundary” section; mention scope only where it changes the interpretation of a claim.
2. **Why does it matter?** Explain the actual cost, lost capability, or system consequence in the target context. Use the authors' causal account, a motivating measurement, or a concrete case as appropriate; there is no fixed requirement for a separate motivation experiment. Do not use the proposed method's speedup as a substitute for motivation.
3. **Why does existing work still fail to resolve it?** Compare the closest work on equivalent objects and conditions. State what each already solves, then identify only the remaining mismatch. Use a table when it makes the routes, strengths, and residual gap easier to compare. Difference is not failure. Attribute a limitation to the authors when it is their characterization; label your own inference as such. Check a prior source directly when the distinction is important.
4. **What is the key idea?** Derive the obstacle from the surviving gap, then explain the insight or transferred principle that could address it and the assumptions it needs. Keep a plausible rationale distinct from an experimentally demonstrated mechanism.
5. **How does the design work?** Trace inputs, state, decisions, execution, outputs, and feedback. Explain how each mechanism addresses a challenge, along with relevant assumptions, costs, alternatives, and failure conditions. Distinguish algorithm from engineering implementation. Do not invent a system representation, control ability, or guarantee absent from the paper.

## Experimental setup and figure/table map

This is a standalone evidence section between Questions 5 and 6, not a replacement for the five-question reasoning chain. Give enough setup to orient the audience: workload/dataset, model/system/hardware where relevant, baselines, metrics, and comparison protocol. Avoid burying the presentation in incidental configuration.

For each figure or table used in the PPT storyline, identify its exact label and state briefly **why the experiment was run** and **what conclusion its result supports**, preserving the authors' intended comparison and scope. If the supplied PPT already selects figures, map those slides to the corresponding paper evidence; flag a missing central figure only when it changes the story. Do not recite each figure's contents, overstate its result, or add broad critique here. Add a brief evidence boundary only when needed to prevent a wrong inference (for example, schematic vs. measured, development vs. held-out evaluation, point estimate vs. significance).

6. **Writing and presentation details.** Review wording, terminology, equations, tables, citations, figure labels, and cross-references. Split issues that change a claim, logic, result, or talk track from minor polish. Give the exact location and concise correction; do not inflate severity.
7. **What is the takeaway?** State what the paper demonstrates, the scope where it holds, and one transferable insight. Keep hypotheses and future work distinct from results.

For all sections, use source-faithful terms and causal relations. Explicitly separate the paper's statements, experimental observations, and your interpretation. Useful distinctions include: a program's written control flow versus reacting to newly discovered information; an opportunity to detect an error versus guaranteed detection; runtime completion versus semantic correctness; a task-level comparison versus a mechanism-isolating ablation; and a development result versus a held-out result. Apply these only where relevant, not as a checklist to force into every paper.

When a final PPT is supplied, match its expression depth, causal explanation, and evidence, not merely its headings. The note should let the user rebuild the substance of the relevant slides without rediscovering the paper. Use natural labels such as “背景”“问题”“动机” where they fit the deck; do not add a generic “汇报主句” or force a one-sentence summary into every section. When the user's later correction changes the abstraction level, revise the whole causal story rather than appending a correction paragraph while leaving the original framing intact. When no user note exists, do not manufacture a correction section. When the note is partial, fill its gaps from the paper without treating blank sections as mistaken claims.
